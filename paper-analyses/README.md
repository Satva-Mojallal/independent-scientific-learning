Published Research Paper Analyses

This folder contains structured analyses of published scientific papers.

The purpose is to understand how researchers move from a biological question to an experimental strategy, how they interpret their results, and whether their conclusions are justified by the evidence.

Reading a paper is not simply about understanding its introduction and conclusion. It is about reconstructing the experimental logic behind the research.

Framework for analysing a paper

For each paper, examine the following:

1. Research question: What did the researchers want to find out?
2. Background: What was already known, and what gap did the study address?
3. Hypothesis: What explanation or mechanism were the researchers investigating?
4. Predictions: What outcomes would be expected if the hypothesis were correct?
5. Experimental design: What did the researchers compare, manipulate, or measure?
6. Methods: Why was each method selected, and what does it directly measure?
7. Controls: What controls were included, and what alternative explanations do they address?
8. Results: What did the experiments actually show?
9. Interpretation: How did the authors interpret their findings?
10. Limitations: What do the results not establish?
11. Alternative explanations: Could another explanation account for the observations?
12. Follow-up experiment: What experiment could address an unresolved question?

Reasoning backwards

An important part of this project is learning to reason backwards from experimental results.

For example:

- Given a Western blot result, what question might the researchers have been asking?
- Why might they have selected Western blotting rather than immunofluorescence?
- What controls would make the result interpretable?
- What conclusion is justified by the data?
- What additional experiment would strengthen the proposed mechanism?

This approach helps develop the ability to understand experimental decisions rather than simply memorize method names.

Attribution and responsible use of research

Analyses should:

- Identify the original paper and provide a citation or DOI.
- Link to the original publication whenever possible.
- Distinguish the authors' stated rationale from my own reconstruction and critique.
- Paraphrase rather than reproduce substantial portions of the paper.
- Check the article's licence before reusing figures or substantial text.
- Avoid presenting the authors' results as experiments personally performed.

Open-access papers are useful because they make the full research article easier to access. However, open access does not automatically mean every figure or element can be reused without restrictions.

Main objective

The aim is to become better at understanding why experiments are performed, how evidence is generated, and whether scientific conclusions are supported by the experimental results.
